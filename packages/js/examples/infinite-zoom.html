Infinite zoom — dssCollapse, compose

The field is a function of position, not an array of samples, so there is no resolution to run out of: evaluating it at 1e-30 costs exactly what evaluating it at 1 costs. That alone does not buy you an infinite zoom, and this page is careful about the difference. A smooth field magnified far enough is correctly featureless — the answer is exact and there is nothing in it. What keeps the picture full at every depth is the collapse law: advance the renormalisation time s = −log(T−t) by log λ and the field returns to exactly what it was, rescaled by λ^−b in space and λ^a in amplitude. Camera and clock advance together, and one period tiles the entire dive.

The receipt is the tile error. Every frame the page takes 48 of the points it is about to draw, samples the field there, and compares them against the same 48 screen points one period earlier — a window λ^b wider, at s − log λ — rescaled by λ^a. That is precisely the claim the dive rests on, so if it fails the dive is a lie. It reads about 1e-14 relative: not 1e-16, because the camera is recomputed from scratch at both ends and exp/pow disagree in the last couple of bits, which is roundoff in the test rig rather than error in the law. loop: broken sets freezeProfile: false, so the wrapped field keeps its own clock and no longer repeats — the number jumps to ~1.1, the difference panel lights up, and you can see the picture snap once per period. clock: off pins the field's time and lets the camera keep diving into a frozen field: the tile error goes to ~2.6 and, more tellingly, detail collapses from 0.99 to 1e-4 within four decades. That is the honest limit of zooming into a fixed analytic field, and it is why the clock has to move.

And the grid. sampling: baked 512³ quantises the sample position to a fixed world lattice — a nearest-neighbour lookup into a volume baked once at 512³, which for three float32 velocity components is 1.6 GB and lasts until magnification 512. The relative error against the analytic value is printed whether the toggle is on or off, so you can watch it climb before you look at it: about 5e-2 at ×1, 9e-1 by ×60, and pinned near 1.3 once the whole view fits inside one voxel. Trilinear filtering would soften the blocks and not change that number. You could instead bake the profile once, in the frame the law rescales, and do the rescaling analytically — that genuinely works, it is worth knowing it works, and it costs you an interpolation error at every sample plus the requirement that your field have an exact rescaling law in the first place.

What is honestly unbounded, and what is not. The depth counter is a log accumulator, so it passes 1e30 with no special case, and the renderer stays in one period by subtracting log λ from s whenever it wraps — which is legitimate only because the loop is exact, which is the number above. The base profile is small: two octave bands composed with compose, k = 1…24 in 18 modes, about 1.4 decades of content. Nothing in it knows about 1e30. What is unbounded is the law that rescales it, and the price of that is repetition — a zoom that stays sharp forever has to repeat, because there is one profile and it is being reused. This page shows you the repetition rather than hiding it behind a cross-fade.
